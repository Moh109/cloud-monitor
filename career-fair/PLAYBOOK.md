## The thing to understand first

You are a junior graduating **May 2028**, so you're targeting **Summer 2027** —
the summer between junior and senior year. That is the single most valuable
internship summer there is, because it's the one that converts to a full-time
offer. Say this out loud at every booth:

> "I graduate in May 2028, so a Summer 2027 internship lines up exactly with your
> full-time pipeline."

Recruiters are measured on return-offer conversion. You just told them you're a
low-risk pick. Most students never make that connection for them.

**Second thing:** the fair is roughly **20% of the outcome**. For nearly every
company here, the online application is the actual gate — the booth conversation
is what makes a recruiter pull your name back out of a pile of 4,000. Which means
the highest-leverage thing in this entire document isn't on fair day at all:

**Apply online to every Tier 1 and Tier 2 company *before* you walk in.**

Then at the booth you get to say *"I applied on the 3rd, req 12345"* instead of
*"where should I apply?"* — and the recruiter can pull you up on the spot. Big
energy companies typically open Summer 2027 postings in late summer/fall and fill
them by roughly November–December. Being early is worth more than being polished.
Verify each company's actual dates on their careers page; don't take my word for
the window.

## The 30-second pitch

Memorize the skeleton, improvise the hook. Do not recite it — a memorized-sounding
pitch is worse than a rough one.

> "Hi, I'm Muhammad — junior in Computer Science at UH, graduating May 2028, and
> I'm looking for a Summer 2027 software engineering internship.
> **[hook]**
> Most recently I built CloudGuard, a scanner that audits an AWS account for
> security misconfigurations, maps each finding to CIS and MITRE ATT&CK, and can
> fail a CI build before a bad config ever ships.
> I'd love to hear what your intern team actually works on."

Ending on a **question** matters. It turns a monologue into a conversation and it
hands them the easy part.

**Hooks by company:**

- **SLB / NOV** — "I'm drawn to SLB specifically because you ship real software products, not just internal IT, and the C++/Python mix is exactly what I've been working in."
- **ExxonMobil / Chevron / ConocoPhillips** — "I've been focused on cloud security and CI/CD automation, which I know is a big piece of what your digital organization is building right now."
- **Sysco** — "I'm interested in the scale problem — you're moving supply-chain data at Fortune 100 volume, and that's a different kind of engineering than people expect when they hear food distribution."
- **Fast Enterprises** — "I like that your interns are on real client implementations early instead of shadowing. I lead a dev team at Code Coogs, so I'm used to owning delivery and a deadline."
- **FBI** — "I hold AttackIQ's MITRE ATT&CK operationalization certification and I've built both a CSPM scanner and a detection-as-code pipeline. I'd like to understand the Honors Internship cyber track and its deadline."
- **CenterPoint** — "I'm interested in the OT side — where grid infrastructure and security actually meet."

### The phone demo — your unfair advantage

Have `samples/sample_report.html` from CloudGuard open in a tab on your phone, or
at minimum the dashboard screenshot.

> "Can I show you fifteen seconds of what it outputs?"

Almost no student does this. A recruiter who has looked at 120 sheets of paper
will remember the one person who showed them a working dashboard.

**Two rules:** only offer it once the conversation is already going well, and only
if the line behind you is short. Never make five people wait while you scroll.

## Questions that separate you

Ask two or three. They signal that you've thought about the job, not just the logo.

1. "What does an intern actually ship over the summer — is there a project they own end to end?"
2. "What's the stack your intern team works in day to day?"
3. "What separates the interns who get return offers from the ones who don't?"
4. "Is there anything in your application process that screens people out unexpectedly?"
5. "When do applications close, and when do decisions typically go out?"
6. "Should I reference this conversation when I apply, or is there a specific req I should target?"

**Never ask:** "So what does your company do?" or "Do you have any internships?"
Both announce that you did zero preparation, and recruiters notice instantly.

**Always close with:** "Could I get your card, or the best email to follow up on?"
Then actually follow up. Which brings us to the part where most people lose.

## "How do I get the internship for sure?"

You can't. Nobody can, and anyone selling you certainty is wrong. What you *can*
do is stack the odds hard enough that the outcome stops being luck.

**The realistic funnel:**

> ~14 conversations → ~8 follow-ups that get replies → 3–5 first-round interviews → **1–2 offers**

That's a good fall. It's achievable from where you're standing.

**Ranked by actual leverage:**

1. **Apply online before the fair.** Nothing else on this list comes close. Energy majors fill Summer 2027 by roughly November–December.
2. **Volume: 25–40 applications this fall, not 8.** The fair covers maybe 12 of them. The most common way strong students end up with nothing is applying only to the companies that showed up to one fair.
3. **Follow up within 24 hours, with something specific in it.**
4. **Lead with CloudGuard every time.** This is your genuine differentiator. Most juniors have coursework; you have a 1,900-line tool with tests, CI, Docker, and a dashboard. That gap is bigger than you think it is.
5. **Work UH alumni on LinkedIn** — five messages a week to UH grads at Tier 1 companies. "I'm a UH CS junior, I applied to X, would you be open to 15 minutes?" Referrals move applications more than career fairs do.
6. **Don't stop at this fair.** The biggest intern classes in the country aren't on this list and don't come to Houston fairs. Apply to them in parallel.

**The one thing that would most change your odds between now and September:**
finish the Detection-as-Code pipeline, or ship one more visible thing on
CloudGuard. Walking up to an ExxonMobil recruiter and saying *"I shipped this last
week"* is a categorically different conversation than *"I built this last
semester."* Recency reads as momentum.

You're in better shape than you probably think. Your resume was already solid, the
project you left off is your best one, and you're hitting this fair in exactly the
right year. The work now is mechanical: apply early, apply widely, rehearse the
pitch until it's boring, and follow up like it's your job.
